Tales of vampires go back to very ancient times. In ancient Babylonia vampires were counted among hundreds of demons, and vampire legends survive in Romania and the Balkans, where Dracula is set, as well as in China, Scotland, Turkey and elsewhere. When plague broke out and ravaged East Prussia in 1710, the Austrian authorities who ruled the region began a systematic investigation of the vampires that had drawn their attention. Once, vampires were even blamed for the disaster, and every grave in a cemetery was dug up.
Two truly remarkable cases occurred during this period. One was the case of the Hungarian peasant Peter Plogojowitz. Plogojowitz died in 1725 and was said to have become a vampire and killed eight people in the small village of Kisilova. The other was the case of the peasant Arnold Paole, who died in 1726 after falling from a hay wagon and likewise became a vampire, killing a great many people and livestock in a Serbian village called Medvegia. It was in the records of the Plogojowitz case that "vanpir", the German word for "vampire", first appeared officially. The Arnold Paole case caused an even greater stir than the Plogojowitz case, and an official inquiry began in December 1731. Thanks to Plogojowitz and Paole, vast quantities of ink were spent all over Europe printing material on the subject.
At the Versailles court of Louis XV, the popular magazine "Le Glaneur" covered the Paole case in detail in its issue of March 3, 1732, and there the French word "vampyre" was used for the first time. On March 11 of the same year, the "London Journal" introduced the word "vampire" to English speakers for the first time.
