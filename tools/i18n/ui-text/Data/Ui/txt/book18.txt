The origins of this vampire fantasy go back to remote antiquity. Many countries have their own accounts of vampires: the Chinese vampire (the jiangshi) is said to work magic by moonlight, and the Mexican vampire is notable for being nothing but bones. A clue can be found on a prehistoric jar discovered in Persia (today's Iran), which shows a man fighting a monster that is trying to suck his blood. The Babylonian myth of Lilitu (a demon known to feed on the blood of infants), comparatively recent at only a few thousand years old, is another clue. Travelers who visited Transylvania (in today's Romania) in the 16th century brought back strange and terrifying tales. Every night, they said, monsters left their lairs to feast on human blood, and these monsters were neither alive nor dead.
Two ideas underlie the vampire legend. First, that evil spirits use corpses for wicked purposes; and second, that some of the dead are so wicked they cannot pass on to the next world and remain in their corpses as vampires. Legends of these monsters - vampires - exist in every country, but most arose in Eastern Europe. Countries such as Romania, Russia, Albania and Moravia have many vampire legends, and the powers they give vampires are just as varied. Having drunk their victims' blood, vampires possess superhuman strength, can change into animals such as bats and wolves, and hold power over the creatures of the night. How one becomes a vampire differs from country to country, but the most common way is to fall victim to a vampire. A victim whose blood is taken by a vampire dies, and in death becomes a vampire too, doomed to roam the night.
