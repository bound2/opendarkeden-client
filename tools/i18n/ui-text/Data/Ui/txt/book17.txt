The excommunicated, suicides, victims of violent death, witches, stillborn children, people denied a Christian burial, people born with teeth, people born wearing a caul (the thin membrane that covers a baby's head at birth), people with eyes too dark or unusually blue, redheads and people with moles were all more likely than others to become vampires, and when such a person died, two precautions were taken between the coffining and the burial. In Romania a nail was driven into the corpse's head or the body was pricked with needles, and it was sometimes smeared with the fat of a pig slaughtered on St. Ignatius' Day. To keep the vampire's soul from returning to its body, a special object was placed in the mouth (a garlic bulb in Romania, a piece of consecrated bread in Greece, a slice of lemon in Saxony in Germany). Poppy seeds or briar thorns were strewn along every road leading to the cemetery so that the vampire would have to pick them up one by one. In Serbia, crosses painted in tar were hung on doors and windows to ward off vampire attacks. In Romania, strings of garlic were hung in every room, and garlic was rubbed on doors, windows, chimneys and keyholes. But the surest method was never to wander alone at night, and never to be bitten by a vampire.
